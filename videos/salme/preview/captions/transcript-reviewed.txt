Under a village in Estonia, archaeologists found two ships buried in the sand. Inside those boats were the remains of 41 men, some bones bore deep cuts. Beside the bodies lay swords, shields, and pieces from a board game, small enough to hold in one hand, then came the date. These men had died decades before the raid often used to mark the start of the Viking age. The story in the ground was older than the famous story on the page. What had brought them across the Baltic so early? On the Scandinavian coast in the 8th century, the water was part of daily life. A family might live from its fields and still need a boat to fish, carry goods or reach another settlement. A sheltered stretch of water could serve as a road. A crossing could save a long trip along the shore. Across the Baltic boats took people farther from home to shores where they could trade and meet strangers. Ships also had a place in how some communities buried their dead. Look at what was laid inside. At Valsgärde, in what is now Sweden, archaeologists found boat graves with finely worked helmets. Giving up a vessel and putting it in a grave could show the standing of the person buried inside. A useful boat took work to replace. That custom would help explain what archaeologists saw on a shore across the Baltic. Off the coast of Northumbria, the island of Lindisfarne was home to a monastic community. At its heart stood the shrine of St. Cuthbert. In the year 793, boats came towards the island. The raiders came ashore and attacked the monastery. They killed members of the community and stripped the monastery of valuables. News reached Alcuin, an English scholar at the Court of Charlemagne across the sea from Northumbria. He wrote about a church stained with blood and wondered what wrongs his own people might have committed to bring such a disaster upon them. His question shows how deeply the news shook him. In the decades that followed, raids spread along the coasts of Britain and Ireland. Other communities faced that danger from the water. Crews could come ashore, seize what they could carry, and be back on the water before help arrived. The coast gave them many places to land. The accounts of Lindisfarne gave historians a date to put on a timeline. From that date, a familiar story begins. The evidence for what came before lay on a shore across the Baltic. In 2008, construction workers at Salme cut into an old shoreline. The ground held something unexpected. They uncovered human bones, sword fragments, and iron rivets. One clue mattered because of where it lay. The rivets helped archaeologists trace a buried boat. Their positions followed a curve. Inside it were seven men. Nearby lay the outline of another vessel. A second, larger ship held 34. The men lay close together inside. Several of the men had died violently. One had taken repeated blows to his upper arm. In the larger ship, the dead had been laid out in layers, one above the next. The uppermost bodies were found with shields placed over them. Whoever buried the men had taken care over where they lay. The burials date to around 750, roughly 40 years before Lindisfarne. An armed crossing of the Baltic had already happened. What had gone wrong on this shore? The graves offered some answers about the men's deaths. Their purpose in coming across the Baltic was harder to recover. In the lap of one man was a set of gaming pieces and three dice. The grave also held finely decorated weapons, made with a care that can still be seen. There were the remains of dogs and birds of prey, too, placed with the dead. Some researchers think the men came to raid. The weapons fit that possibility. Others have suggested that they were part of a delegation. People sent to negotiate could travel with armed guards and costly possessions. They might have gone ashore expecting to deliver a message or make a deal. Either kind of journey could have ended in a fight. The bones give us a glimpse of that fight. The purpose of the voyage is still an open question. The place it started was something the researchers could investigate in the lab. They needed something that had formed before the men set out. The clue lay in the men's teeth. As a child's teeth form, the enamel preserves chemical traces of the food and water they grow up with. Researchers compared traces in the men's teeth with measurements from different regions. That evidence, along with the objects in the graves, pointed back across the Baltic towards central Sweden. Then a DNA study found something more personal. It showed how some of the men were related. Four men lying side-by-side in the larger ship were brothers. Someone had buried them together. A voyage like this began well before anyone took up an oar. It took people who could shape timber, make fittings and gather supplies. Someone had to stock the boat with food and equipment for a long crossing. Once afloat, it depended on people who knew how to sail, how to row, and when to land. To see how far those crews could go, start with what the shipbuilders left behind. Take a plank. Lay the edge of the next one over it and fasten them together with iron rivets. Repeat the joint along the hull. The overlap helped make the hull strong while keeping it light. With relatively little of the hull below the water, a ship could come close to a beach or enter a shallow river. On open water, the sail did much of the work. The wind could spare the rowers some effort. When the wind dropped, rowers could keep the ship moving. Oars helped the crew steer through narrow water. Builders adapted the same method for different jobs. A long narrow warship had room for rows of oars. A broader cargo ship had space for goods with room for bundles and containers that could be carried from port to port. That gave crews choices when they reached a new coast. A beach could become a landing place. A river mouth could be the way to a market deep inland. At that market, cargo could pass to another boat or another trader. Some crews followed the Volga towards markets tied to the Islamic world, Traveling far beyond the Baltic coast. Others took the Dnieper south to the Black Sea and on to Constantinople. Furs and Amber moved through these networks. Silver reached Scandinavia in coins called dirhams. Traders could weigh the coins and trade them for their silver value. An unfamiliar coin could still be worth its weight in silver. A small bronze Buddha found on the Swedish island of Helgö gives a sense of how far an object could travel. The figure was probably made in the Swat Valley in what is now Pakistan. It could have changed hands many times before it reached a house in Sweden. Its route is still unknown. People were forced along these routes too. The same ships that carried goods could carry captives. Raiders captured men, women and children, and traders sold them far from home. Some of the wealth that made voyages profitable came from those sales. For those taken, a voyage could mean permanent separation. In 865, a Viking army arrived in England. The scale had changed. The following year, it took York. Some of the newcomers stayed. And, under Scandinavian rule, York grew into a busy trading city. Beneath a street called Coppergate, archaeologists found timber buildings, leather shoes, and waste left by people who worked metal. A city taken by an army had become a place to make a living. The leather shoe brings us closer to those who walked its streets. Two centuries later, the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada crossed the sea to claim the English throne. He was killed at Stamford Bridge near York in 1066. His claim ended there. That defeat is often used to mark the end of the Viking age in England. Another date entered the story. The people who wrote about Lindisfarne recorded what the raid had cost their community. We can read that history because their words survived. The men at Salme remain nameless. We can still see where someone chose to lay four brothers together and the things they put beside them. One small piece from a board game lay there for more than a thousand years. When a story begins with a date, ask whose account gave us that date. Then, look for the people who were there before anyone wrote the story down.
